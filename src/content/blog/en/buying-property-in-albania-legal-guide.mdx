Most of the property disputes that reach my office do not start with fraud. They start with a purchase made in a hurry: the buyer saw the apartment, agreed a price and went to the notary. The notary did their job, which is to verify the identity of the parties and the certificate of ownership. Nobody checked the history of the property, because nobody was asked to.

This guide covers what a foreign buyer can acquire in Albania, the checks I run before every purchase, the steps from deposit to registration, and the mistakes I see most often.

## What a foreigner can buy

A foreign natural person can freely buy apartments and buildings. The restrictions concern **land**.

*Law No. 7980/1995 "On the sale and purchase of land plots"* grants foreigners the right to buy land only in connection with an investment: under *Article 5*, the right arises once the investment carried out under the building permit reaches at least three times the value of the plot; until then the plot is leased. *Article 7* excludes from sale to foreigners land of archaeological or historical value, national parks and military areas. Agricultural land, forests and pastures cannot be sold to foreign persons at all; they can only be leased.

In practice:

- **An apartment in Tirana, Durrës, Vlora or Saranda**: bought directly, with a passport, no residence permit required.
- **A house with a garden or a villa with land**: the plot under the building is the delicate part. The solution depends on the case and must be examined before any deposit is paid.
- **Building land or agricultural land**: not available to a foreign natural person directly. Structures through an Albanian company are possible but require specific assessment.

## Five checks before the notary

**The property card (kartela) and the map.** The card issued by the State Cadastre Agency (ASHK) shows the current owner, the surface, the origin of title and, above all, the **encumbrances**: mortgages, seizures, restrictions, registered leases. Ask for a card issued in the last few days. A registered mortgage follows the property, not the seller.

**The origin of title.** Purchase, inheritance, legalisation, court judgment: each has its weak points. Legalised buildings often show surface differences; inherited property may have unregistered heirs; land allocated under the 1991 agrarian reform is the main source of overlapping titles. I always ask for the seller's acquisition deed, not just the certificate.

**The seller's marital status.** Property acquired during marriage belongs to both spouses even when the card names only one. A sale without the other spouse's signature is among the most frequent grounds for annulment claims.

**The status of the building.** For new buildings: building permit, occupancy certificate and registration of the building at the cadastre. Without the occupancy certificate the building is not registered and your apartment stays in limbo until the developer resolves it. For older buildings: legalisation, unregistered alterations, matching surfaces.

**Debts attached to the property.** Utilities, building management fees, local taxes. They are not legal encumbrances, but they become your practical problem from day one. Ask for proof of settlement before signing.

## The steps of a purchase

**Preliminary contract and deposit.** When the sale cannot close immediately, the parties sign a preliminary contract with a deposit. *Article 601 of the Albanian Civil Code* defines the deposit as a sum given to confirm the contract and secure its performance; *Article 602* sets the consequences: a buyer who withdraws without cause loses the deposit, a seller who withdraws returns double. The preliminary contract must state the deadline, the price and the conditions to be met before the final deed. I recommend signing it before a notary as well.

**The notarial deed.** *Article 83 of the Civil Code* leaves no room: the transfer of ownership of immovable property is made by notarial deed and registered, otherwise it is not valid. Private agreements and verbal promises transfer nothing. *Article 750* repeats the rule specifically for sales.

**Payment.** The price is paid through a bank, not in cash. Anti-money-laundering legislation requires this for real estate transactions and the notary records the method of payment in the deed. For the buyer, the bank transfer is also the best evidence.

**Transfer tax.** The seller pays the tax on the gain from the transfer, 15% of the difference between the sale price and the acquisition value, before registration; without that payment the cadastre does not register the transfer. The law places this cost on the seller, but the parties often negotiate otherwise, so settle it in the preliminary contract.

**Registration at the ASHK.** Under *Article 193 of the Civil Code*, the contract transferring ownership is entered in the immovable property register. Until then you have a contract; afterwards you have ownership enforceable against everyone. The notary usually submits the deed; ask for the application number and follow it until the new certificate is issued.

## Buying off-plan

A contract for an apartment not yet built concerns a future asset. *Article 706 of the Civil Code* provides that ownership is acquired when the asset comes into existence. Until the building is completed and registered you are not an owner but a creditor of the developer. Your protection depends entirely on the contract: delivery deadline, penalties for delay, payment in instalments against progress of works and, above all, a guarantee in case the building is never completed.

Before the first instalment, verify the building permit and the developer's title to the land. A developer building on someone else's plot under a construction agreement cannot sell you more than that agreement gives them.

## When the surface does not match

The contract says 85 m², the survey gives 79. *Article 752 of the Civil Code* entitles the buyer to a price reduction when the sale is made at a price per unit of measure and the actual surface is smaller. The protection only works if the contract states the price per square metre, not just the total.

## Buying without travelling to Albania

A buyer can act through a power of attorney. A power of attorney executed before a foreign notary needs an apostille and a certified Albanian translation; the alternative is a power of attorney issued at an Albanian consulate. It must describe the property and the powers precisely, otherwise the Albanian notary will not accept it. In most cases the practical route is a power of attorney at home and the lawyer's presence in Tirana at the deed.

## Three recurring mistakes

**The deposit paid before the checks.** The deposit comes after the card and the origin of title have been examined, not before. Recovering it once problems appear is harder than *Article 602* suggests.

**A price in the deed lower than the real one.** The "double price" practice, used to reduce the seller's tax, leaves the buyer without proof of the amount paid and with a lower tax base when they sell. The risk is entirely the buyer's.

**Not following the registration.** A notarial deed without registration is half the job. As explained in the article on [registering property in Albania](/en/blog/how-to-register-property-in-albania), every delay makes the chain harder to repair.

The checks described here usually take a few working days and cost far less than any litigation over a defective title. If you are planning a purchase in Albania, [contact the firm](/en/contact) before paying a deposit, not after.
